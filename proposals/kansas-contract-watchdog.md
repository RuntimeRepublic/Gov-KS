# Why Runtime Republic Needs a Kansas Contract Watchdog

When Kansas signs a contract, taxpayers should be able to see what the government promised to buy, who approved it, how much has been paid, and what the vendor delivered. Those answers can be scattered across meeting packets, committee testimony, payment records, and documents citizens must request themselves. By the time a missed deadline makes the news, taxpayers may have lost years of opportunities to ask questions.

The KLISS modernization project shows why Runtime Republic needs a permanent contract register. Public records confirm that the Legislature’s contract with Propylon was signed in October 2022 and that the planned June 2025 deployment was missed. Our research still needs the signed agreement, any executed addenda, and payment records that show what taxpayers received for the money spent. The same questions should apply to routine renewals, local construction projects, and contracts that deliver exactly what they promised.

Runtime Republic should organize this work through a Kansas nonprofit operating as a **501(c)(4) civic league**. That fits two goals. We want supporters to contribute without receiving a charitable tax deduction. We also want the freedom to take documented problems to lawmakers and ask them to change the rules. Publishing what happened is research. Asking legislators to vote for a specific reform is lobbying. Both belong in this effort.

The Node-Based Budget Framework gives us a clear standard. Tie each contract to its authorizing budget, identify the human owner, and record the spending ceiling and promised results. Every four months, show what was paid, what was accepted, and what decision officials made about continued funding. When a material problem surfaces, flag it immediately. Taxpayers should be able to see what officials did while there’s still time to act.

Start with KLISS, one routine state renewal, and one local capital project. Publish the sources, mark the gaps, and correct the record when new evidence arrives. Then use what we learn to propose rules that make timely disclosure and review standard practice across Kansas. A public contract should leave a trail any taxpayer can follow from the first vote to the final dollar.
